Maryam Nazem

Computational biomechanics — building subject-specific models of bone and joints, and establishing when their predictions can be trusted.

I build computer models of bone and joints from medical imaging, and I test how far their predictions can be relied upon for decisions that matter: fracture risk, implant design, and the evidence regulators accept.

Simulation is only as good as the judgements behind it, most of which never get written down. My work makes those judgements visible, and checks the answers against what can actually be measured.

Research

Predicting femoral bone strength after spinal cord injury PhD research · Biomedical Engineering, NJIT–Rutgers joint program · [advisor and lab]

Spinal cord injury causes rapid bone loss below the level of injury, and the fragility fractures that follow are common, serious, and hard to anticipate. The clinical standard for assessing bone — DXA — struggles in exactly this population, and it measures density rather than strength, which is not the same thing.

I am building subject-specific finite element models of the proximal femur from CT imaging across a cohort of roughly fifty bones, predicting mechanical strength directly and comparing those predictions against the clinical measurements taken from the same individuals. The question is whether a model can tell a clinician something the standard scan cannot. Alongside this I collect movement data in the motion capture laboratory, which connects what happens at the whole-body level to the loads a bone actually sees.

Benchmarking the credibility of computational knee models NIH KneeHub consortium · University of Denver

Two competent teams given the same imaging and the same measurements will build models that disagree — not through error, but through the hundreds of small judgements modelling requires. Where to place a boundary in an ambiguous scan. How stiff to make a ligament. How much tension it carries at rest.

Within KneeHub I led the benchmarking phase, which asked the question the project existed to answer: can models predict loading conditions they were never calibrated against? Answering it meant first building a framework in which five teams' incompatible outputs could be compared honestly, then quantifying how far their predictions drift once they leave familiar ground. The work bears on how computational evidence is assessed under ASME V&V 40, the standard the FDA references for medical device submissions.

Code and data

The benchmarking framework and the Abaqus automation tooling developed for the KneeHub work are being prepared for public release with a citable DOI. Reproducibility is the point of the research; withholding the code would be inconsistent with it.
